Hiring

How to hire a contractor you can trust

The contractor you pick shapes the price, the schedule and how the job feels from start to finish. A few checks before you sign, most of them free and online, protect your money and your home.

10 min readUpdated October 2026United States

Most remodeling problems trace back to the start of the job: a vague scope, a large deposit, a missing license, or a handshake where a contract should be. The rules for home improvement work differ from state to state, but the basics below apply almost everywhere, and knowing them puts you on equal footing with anyone you interview.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Rules change, so confirm the details with your state's contractor licensing board or consumer protection office. We use California as an example in a few places because its rules are detailed and well documented.

1. Check the license first

Contractor licensing is handled state by state. Some states license remodelers and general contractors at the state level. Some leave it to cities or counties. A few have little or no licensing for remodeling work, though they may still license trades like electrical and plumbing, or require contractors to register. Start by finding out which applies where you live: search for your state's contractor licensing board, and check with your city or county if the state doesn't license remodelers.

Ask for the license or registration number up front, then look it up yourself. A contractor who hesitates to give it is telling you something. When the record comes up, look at:

  • Status. It should say the license is current and active. Expired, suspended or inactive licenses are a stop sign.
  • Business name. It should match the name on the bid and the contract.
  • Type of license. Make sure it covers the work you're hiring for, such as general building or a specific trade.
  • Complaints and discipline. Many boards show public complaints or legal actions on the record.

In California, for example, most home improvement work has to be done by a contractor licensed by the Contractors State License Board (CSLB). You can check a license at cslb.ca.gov by license number, business name or personal name. A "B" general building contractor can manage projects that involve several trades, while specialty licenses start with "C," such as C-10 for electrical or C-36 for plumbing. The record also shows the contractor's bond and either a workers' compensation policy or an exemption, which is only allowed when the contractor has no employees. Salespeople who sell home improvement contracts must also be registered with the CSLB.

2. Get comparable bids

Two or three bids are usually enough, as long as they price the same job. If one contractor quotes quartz counters and soft-close drawers and another quotes laminate, the lower number tells you nothing.

The fix is a written scope. Write down what you want done, room by room, with the finishes you have in mind, and give the same document to everyone. Ask each contractor to price it line by line and to list what they are leaving out, such as permits, design, haul-away or appliance installation.

Be careful with a bid that is much lower than the rest. It may leave out work that will come back later as change orders, or it may come from someone cutting corners on permits or insurance. Before you compare, it helps to know what a fair range looks like for your area.

3. The contract and your right to cancel

Get a written contract for any remodeling job, even where your state doesn't require one. Many states do require a written contract for home improvement work and spell out what it must contain. Read it all before you sign, and don't sign anything with blank spaces. A good contract usually includes:

  • The contractor's name, business address and license or registration number
  • A detailed description of the work and the materials, including brands or models where they matter
  • The total contract price and any finance charges
  • The down payment, if any, and a schedule of progress payments tied to specific work
  • The approximate start date and the date the work should be substantially complete
  • Who pulls the permits and who pays the fees
  • Any notices your state requires, such as information about liens and your right to cancel
  • A statement that changes to the work will be made in writing

Your right to cancel. If you sign a contract in your home, you may have the right to cancel it within a few days. The Federal Trade Commission's Cooling-Off Rule gives you three business days to cancel many sales made at your home over a small amount, and the seller has to tell you about that right in writing. The rule has exceptions, and many states have their own cancellation rules for home improvement contracts that can add to it. Your state attorney general or consumer protection office can tell you what applies.

In California, for example, state law requires a written contract for most home improvement jobs and spells out much of what it must contain, including the salesperson's name and registration number if one sold the job. For most home improvement contracts signed in your home, California gives you three business days to cancel, and longer periods can apply in some cases, including for some homeowners aged 65 and older.

If you decide to cancel, follow the instructions in the notice and keep a copy of what you send.

At HRG, every project starts with a written scope and a written contract, so you know what is included before work begins. Whoever you hire, ask for the same.

4. Deposits and progress payments

Keep the deposit modest, and tie every payment after it to work that's done. Some states cap how much a contractor can collect up front. In California, for example, the down payment on a home improvement contract can't be more than 10 percent of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is less. Check whether your state has a limit.

After the down payment, payments should follow the work. Each progress payment should match work that is done or materials that have been delivered to your home. The contract should describe each stage in plain terms, like "rough plumbing and electrical complete and inspected," so you both know when a payment is due.

Payments you can trace protect you if there is ever a dispute. Avoid paying in cash, and make checks out to the business named on the license, not to an individual.

5. Change orders in writing

Almost every remodel changes along the way. You pick a different tile, or demolition turns up rotted framing that has to be replaced. That's normal. What matters is how the change is handled.

Each change should be written up as a change order before the work is done. It should describe the change, the added or reduced cost, and any effect on the schedule, and both of you should sign it. Verbal "while we're at it" requests are where many budget disputes start. If it isn't written down, it is hard to sort out later.

6. Mechanics liens and lien waivers

Every state has mechanics lien laws, though the details and deadlines differ. In general, they let the people who work on or supply materials for your project file a lien against your property if they aren't paid. That can include subcontractors and suppliers you never hired directly. The uncomfortable part is that in many states a lien can be filed even if you paid your contractor in full, when the contractor didn't pay them.

In some states, including California, subcontractors and suppliers send preliminary notices early in the job. They aren't accusations. They just mean those companies are working on your project and are preserving their rights. Keep them, because they tell you who you need waivers from.

Lien waivers, also called lien releases, are how you protect yourself. When you make a payment, ask for a signed waiver from the contractor and from the subcontractors and suppliers who were paid from it. A conditional waiver takes effect once the payment clears; an unconditional waiver confirms payment was received. Some states require specific waiver forms. Before the final payment, collect unconditional waivers from everyone who worked on or supplied the job.

Waivers before the final check

Don't make the last payment until you have lien waivers covering the work. If a contractor resists, ask why. Some homeowners pay subcontractors and suppliers with joint checks to make sure the money reaches them.

7. Insurance

Ask for certificates of insurance for two kinds of coverage, and ask the insurance company or agent to send them directly to you.

  • General liability covers damage to your home or your neighbor's property caused by the work.
  • Workers' compensation covers injuries to the contractor's workers. If a worker without coverage is hurt on your property, you could face a claim. Rules on who must carry it vary by state. In California, the CSLB license record shows it.

Check the dates on each certificate and make sure the business name matches the contract. It is also worth calling your homeowners insurance company before a big project. Some policies have conditions about construction or vacant homes.

8. Red flags

Any one of these is a reason to slow down. Two or more is a reason to walk away.

  • Cash only, or a discount for paying in cash
  • A deposit larger than your state allows, or a request to pay most of the job up front
  • No written contract, or a contract with blank spaces
  • Pressure to sign today, or a price that is "only good right now"
  • Door-to-door offers, especially after fires, storms or other disasters, when unlicensed operators often show up
  • A license number that doesn't match the business name, or no license number at all where your state requires one
  • A suggestion that you pull the permit yourself as the owner, so the contractor doesn't have to
  • No physical business address, or only a cell phone number

State licensing boards and attorneys general often publish warnings about common scams, particularly after disasters. If something feels off, check their sites before you sign.

9. Questions to ask before you sign

  • What is your license or registration number, and what work does it cover?
  • Can you have your insurer send me your general liability and workers' comp certificates?
  • Who will be on site day to day, and who do I call with questions?
  • Which parts of the job will subcontractors do?
  • Who pulls the permit, and are permit fees included in this price?
  • What is not included in this bid?
  • How do you handle change orders and hidden conditions?
  • What is the payment schedule, and what work does each payment match?
  • Will you provide lien waivers with each payment?
  • How long until you can start, and how long will the work take?
  • What warranty do you give on your work, and is it in writing?
  • Can I talk to homeowners whose projects you finished recently?

Good contractors expect these questions and answer them without fuss. The way someone responds tells you a lot about how the job will go.

Common questions

How many bids should I get?

Two or three is usually enough, as long as everyone prices the same written scope. More bids rarely help if they're for different versions of the job.

Can a contractor ask for half the price up front?

It depends on your state. Some states cap the down payment on home improvement work. In California, for example, it can't be more than 10 percent of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is less. Wherever you live, a large payment before work starts puts your money at risk, and later payments should follow completed work.

What if my state doesn't license remodelers?

Check with your city or county, which may license or register contractors. Either way, lean harder on the other checks: insurance certificates sent by the insurer, references from recent jobs, a detailed written contract and a payment schedule tied to finished work.

Should I pull the permit myself to save time?

Generally, no. An owner-builder permit can make you responsible for the work and, in some cases, for the workers. If you're hiring a contractor, the contractor should pull the permit.

What if a contractor I hired has a problem with their license later?

Check the license again before work starts and before big payments. If you have a dispute you can't resolve, your state's licensing board or consumer protection office can tell you how to file a complaint and what your options are.
