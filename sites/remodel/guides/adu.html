ADUs and conversions

ADUs and garage conversions: what to know before you build

A second home on your lot can house a parent, a grown child or a tenant. There are a few common ways to build one, and each comes with its own rules, costs and timeline. Here's how they compare, who sets the rules, and what to check before you start.

10 min readUpdated October 2026United States

Accessory dwelling units, or ADUs, are allowed in more places than they used to be. California, Oregon, Washington and a growing number of other states have passed laws that make them easier to build, and many cities have loosened their own rules. Still, what you can build depends heavily on where you live. The shape of your yard, where the sewer line runs and whether you're on a slope can matter as much as the law itself.

The kinds of ADU

An ADU is a self-contained home with its own kitchen, bathroom and entrance, built on the same lot as a house. Some places also allow a smaller unit carved out of the house you already have. California calls this a junior ADU, or JADU; other places may call it an interior ADU or not allow it as a separate category. Here's how the common options line up:

TypeWhere it goesSize, generallyGood fit when
Detached ADUA new building in the backyardCapped by state or local rules, which vary widelyYou have yard space and want the most privacy
Attached ADUAn addition joined to the house, with its own entranceOften limited relative to the size of the houseThe yard is small or the house sits well on the lot
Garage conversionAn existing garage turned into a living unitThe size of the garage, sometimes with a small additionYou have a garage you rarely park in
Interior unit (a JADU in California)Space inside the existing houseSmall; in California, up to 500 sq ftYou have a spare bedroom or wing near an outside wall

A detached ADU usually costs the most because it needs a new foundation, structure, roof and utility lines. A garage conversion reuses walls, a slab and a roof that are already there, which is why it's often the least expensive way to add a rentable unit. An interior unit needs no new foundation at all, but it's small, and the rules for its kitchen and entrance depend on where you live.

How many units you can add depends on your state and city. Some places allow only one ADU per lot. Others allow more, or allow an ADU plus an interior unit.

Who sets the rules

ADU rules are set by states and cities. In states with a strong ADU law, the state sets a floor: cities can add their own standards but can't go below it. In states without one, your city or county decides almost everything, including whether ADUs are allowed at all on your lot.

Local rules usually cover things like:

  • Which zones allow ADUs, and how many units a lot can have
  • Maximum size and height
  • Setbacks from property lines
  • Parking requirements
  • Whether the owner has to live on the property
  • Whether short-term rentals are allowed
  • Design standards, such as matching the main house
  • Impact fees and utility connection charges
Check your state and your city

ADU laws change often, especially in states that have passed new ones recently, and cities write their own ordinances on top. Before you commit to a design, look up your state's ADU law, if it has one, and check the current rules with your city or county planning department.

An example of a strong state law: California

California's ADU law is one of the most developed in the country, so it's a useful picture of what a strong state law can do. These rules apply in California only. As of this writing, California law generally:

  • Allows an ADU on lots zoned for single-family homes that already have, or will have, a house
  • Allows a JADU of up to 500 sq ft within the walls of the existing home
  • Allows one ADU and one JADU on many single-family lots
  • Requires cities to accept side and rear setbacks of 4 feet for a new detached or attached ADU
  • Doesn't require replacement parking when a garage is converted to an ADU or torn down to build one
  • Doesn't require the owner to live on the property for an ADU
  • Requires the owner to live in the house or the JADU only when the JADU shares a bathroom with the main house
  • Requires cities to approve or deny a complete ADU application within 60 days
  • Bars impact fees on ADUs under 750 sq ft

California cities layer their own standards on top: height limits, design rules, maximum sizes and where the unit can go on the lot. These local rules can't block a basic ADU, but they do shape what you can build. Other states' laws differ in the details, so don't assume any of these points apply where you live.

What to check about your lot

Two houses on the same street can have very different ADU projects. Before you pay for design, look at these:

  • Utilities. Where the electric service, water line and gas line come in, and how far they'd have to run to reach the new unit. Long runs add cost.
  • Sewer or septic. On public sewer, the question is whether the existing line can take another kitchen and bathroom and how the new unit will tie in. On a septic system, the system may need to be evaluated or upgraded, and the local health department may be involved.
  • Slope. A sloped yard can mean retaining walls, grading and extra engineering.
  • Easements. Utility or access easements can rule out part of the yard. Your title report or a survey will show them.
  • Trees. Some cities protect certain trees, and large trees near the building area can limit where a foundation can go.
  • Wildfire, flood and hillside rules. Lots in wildfire areas may need fire-resistant construction, lots in flood zones may face elevation rules, and some cities add hillside requirements such as road access or fire sprinklers.
  • Historic districts and HOAs. Design review can apply to what's visible from the street, and HOA rules may limit or shape an ADU. In some states, the law limits how far an HOA can go.

A short feasibility visit, before any drawings, usually answers most of these questions.

The stages of an ADU project

Every ADU moves through the same five stages. The design and permit stages often take as long as construction.

1. Feasibility. A site visit and a look at zoning, setbacks, utilities and any special rules for your lot. You come out of it knowing what size and type of unit fits, and roughly what it might cost.

2. Design. A designer or architect draws the unit, and a structural engineer adds the structural design. You'll usually also need energy code paperwork (in California, Title 24 calculations), and on some lots a survey or a soils report. This is when you choose the layout and finishes. Some cities offer pre-approved ADU plans that can shorten this stage and plan review.

3. Plan review. The city or county reviews the drawings and usually sends back a list of corrections. Your designer answers them and resubmits. Some garage conversions and smaller ADUs can move through faster review; larger or more complex units go through full plan review, which can take a few months with corrections.

4. Utilities. Utility work often runs alongside plan review: applying for any new service or meter, sizing the electrical panel and planning the sewer connection. Utility companies run on their own schedules, so it pays to start early.

5. Construction. Foundation, framing, roof, rough-in of plumbing, electrical and HVAC, insulation, drywall and finishes, with inspections along the way. A detached ADU typically takes several months to build; a garage conversion or interior unit is usually shorter. From the first visit to move-in, plan on six months to a year for a detached ADU, and less for a conversion.

Utility connections and separate meters

An ADU needs electricity, water and a sewer or septic connection, and often gas. You have two basic choices for each: share the main house's service, or set up a separate one.

Sharing is simpler and usually costs less up front. The trade-off is that you'll need another way to split the bills if you rent the unit. Separate meters let a tenant pay their own utilities, but they can add connection charges, new service lines and wait time from the utility.

Whether a separate connection is required depends on your city and your utilities. In California, for example, state law generally doesn't let a city require a new or separate utility connection for a JADU or for an ADU built inside an existing house or garage, while a new detached ADU may need one. Ask your local utilities early what they require and what they charge.

Don't forget the electrical panel. Adding a kitchen, a bathroom and heating and cooling often pushes an older panel past its capacity, and an upgrade is common.

Renting it out and property tax

An ADU can usually be rented long-term, and for many owners rent is the reason to build. A few things to know:

  • Many cities restrict short-term rentals of ADUs, and some set a minimum lease length. Don't plan on a vacation rental without checking.
  • In most places, an ADU can't be sold separately from the main house.
  • Some cities require the owner to live on the property if an ADU is rented. Check before you build.
  • Local rent and tenant protections may apply once someone moves in. Check the rules for your city before you sign a lease.
  • Talk to your insurance agent before construction starts and again before a tenant moves in.

On property tax, adding an ADU generally adds value to your home, and your assessment can go up. How much depends on your state's property tax rules. In California, for example, generally only the new construction is assessed, and the existing house keeps its current assessed value. Your county assessor can tell you how it works for your property, and a tax professional can tell you how rental income affects your taxes.

Unpermitted units

If you already have a converted garage or back unit that was built without permits, don't assume you can just start renting it. Many cities have paths to legalize existing units, but the work has to be inspected and may need upgrades first. Our permit guide explains why unpermitted work matters.

Prefab or site-built

A site-built ADU is framed on your lot, piece by piece. A prefab ADU is built partly or fully in a factory, then delivered and set on a foundation your contractor prepares. Prefab can shorten time on site and makes some costs more predictable, while site-built gives more freedom with odd lot shapes, slopes and custom layouts. Either way, you still need permits, a foundation, utility connections and inspections, and good access to the backyard matters more for prefab. Common questions

Can I build an ADU on my lot?

It depends on your state and city. In states with strong ADU laws, most single-family lots can have at least one. Elsewhere, local zoning decides. Your city or county planning department can tell you what applies to your lot.

What is a JADU?

JADU stands for junior accessory dwelling unit. It's a California term for a small unit of up to 500 sq ft created inside an existing home. Other states may allow something similar under a different name, or not at all.

Do I lose my parking if I convert the garage?

You lose the covered spaces, so think about where cars will go, including a tenant's. Whether you have to replace that parking depends on where you live. California, for example, generally doesn't require it; other places may.

Do I have to live on the property?

It depends on your state and city. Some places require the owner to live in the main house or the ADU; others, including California for most ADUs, generally don't. Rules have changed over the years, so check what applies to you today.

How long does an ADU take?

Timelines vary. A garage conversion or interior unit often takes several months from start to finish. A detached ADU commonly takes six months to a year, with design and plan review making up a large share of that.
