Permits and inspections

Remodeling permits: what needs one and how it works

Permits can feel like paperwork standing between you and your project. In practice, they're the record that the work was done safely and to code. Here's who issues them, what usually needs one, and how the process tends to go, wherever you live in the U.S.

10 min readUpdated October 2026United States

Most homeowners only deal with a building department a few times in their lives. The details change from place to place, but the pieces are much the same everywhere: who issues the permit, which codes apply, how much review the work needs, and when the inspector comes out.

Who issues your permit

In most of the country, permits come from a local building department. If you live inside city limits, that's usually the city. If you live in an unincorporated area, it's usually the county. In some states, a state agency issues permits and does inspections where no local department does, which is common in rural areas. Some states also handle certain trades, such as electrical work, at the state level.

Your mailing address doesn't always tell you which office is yours. A home with a big-city address can sit outside that city's limits. Two examples of how this looks in large cities: in Los Angeles, the Department of Building and Safety issues building permits inside city limits, while nearby independent cities and unincorporated county areas use their own offices. In Chicago, the Department of Buildings handles permits for the city.

Your property tax bill or the county assessor's records will show whether a parcel is inside a city. The building department's website is the place to confirm current requirements before you start.

What usually needs a permit

The general rule: if the work changes plumbing, electrical, gas, structure, the roof or the building's footprint, it needs a permit. Cosmetic work usually doesn't. These are typical answers across much of the country; your local building department has the final say.

A few items surprise people. A same-size window replacement needs a permit in some places because new windows have to meet energy rules. Decks, pools, retaining walls and taller fences or masonry walls often need permits too, depending on their height. When in doubt, ask the building department or your contractor before work starts, not after.

Over-the-counter permits and plan review

Not every permit needs drawings. Departments sort work by how much review it needs, and that decides how fast you get your permit.

Over-the-counter or express permits cover simple, like-for-like work with no plans: a water heater, a re-roof, a same-size window swap, a panel upgrade or an HVAC change-out. Many departments issue these online or at the counter, often the same day.

Quick review is for small projects with simple drawings, such as a modest bathroom layout change. Some departments review these at the counter or on a short timeline.

Full plan review is for additions, ADUs, structural work and larger layout changes. You submit full drawings, often with structural calculations and energy paperwork, and wait for review. The first review can take weeks, and each round of corrections adds more. Larger projects can take a few months from first submittal to permit.

Review times vary a lot from place to place and from month to month. Projects in historic districts, flood zones or wildfire areas usually take longer, because more people look at them.

Building codes and energy rules

Building codes set the minimum standards for safe construction. Most states and cities don't write their own from scratch. They adopt model codes and then add local amendments.

The residential code. Most of the country uses a version of the International Residential Code, or IRC, for one- and two-family homes. It covers structure, fire safety, stairs, egress windows and much more. States and cities adopt it on their own schedules and change parts of it, so the version in force in your town may not be the newest one.

Energy codes. Most states adopt a version of the International Energy Conservation Code, or IECC, sometimes with changes. California writes its own energy standards, known as Title 24. Either way, the energy code sets rules for insulation, windows, air sealing, lighting, water heating and heating and cooling, and it applies to new space and to many alterations.

In practice, this means a few things for a remodel. New windows may need to meet energy performance requirements. Additions and ADUs usually need energy calculations or a checklist as part of plan review. Some places require testing, such as a duct leakage test, when heating and cooling equipment or ductwork is replaced or added.

Codes are updated on a regular cycle, so requirements change over time. Your designer or contractor should know which versions apply where you live when you submit.

Inspections and the inspection card

A permit isn't the finish line. Each permit comes with a set of inspections, and the work isn't complete in the eyes of the building department until the last one passes.

When the permit is issued, you'll often get an inspection card, or job card, that stays on site. Inspectors sign it, or record results online, as each stage passes. Common inspections include foundation, framing, rough plumbing, rough electrical, mechanical, insulation, drywall in some places, and the final.

The order matters. Rough inspections happen while walls are still open, so the inspector can see what's inside. If a wall gets closed up before its inspection, it may have to be opened again. Your contractor schedules inspections and should be there when the inspector arrives.

  • Keep the inspection card somewhere safe and visible on site
  • Ask for a copy of the final sign-off, or confirm it shows online, before the last payment
  • For an ADU or addition, ask whether a certificate of occupancy is required before anyone moves in
  • Keep permit records with your house papers; you'll want them when you sell
Permits can expire

A permit usually expires if work doesn't start, or stalls for too long without a passed inspection. If your project pauses for months, ask the building department how to keep the permit active.

Why unpermitted work matters

Skipping a permit can look like a way to save time and money. It often costs more later.

When you sell. Many states require sellers to disclose what they know about the property, and some disclosure forms ask directly about work done without permits. Buyers' inspectors and appraisers often spot it. An unpermitted bathroom or garage conversion can lead to price cuts, repair requests, or a lender that won't count that space.

When you insure. If a fire or leak traces back to unpermitted electrical or plumbing work, the claim can get complicated. Talk to your insurance agent if you know of unpermitted work in your home.

When you remodel again. Building departments can require old unpermitted work to be brought up to code, or opened up for inspection, when you apply for a new permit that touches it.

Buying a house with unpermitted work

Before you close, compare the house to its permit history. Many departments let you look up permits by address. A room or unit with no permit record is worth asking about before you buy.

Owner-builder permits

Many places let homeowners pull permits for work on their own home as an owner-builder. It can make sense for small jobs you'll do yourself. It also shifts a lot of responsibility to you, and the rules vary. Some places require a licensed electrician or plumber for certain work even when the owner pulls the building permit.

  • You're responsible for the work meeting code and passing inspection.
  • If you hire workers directly, you may be treated as their employer, with responsibility for workers' compensation insurance and payroll taxes.
  • Owner-builder permits are generally meant for your own home, not for building to sell. Selling soon after the work is finished can raise questions about that.
  • Some lenders and insurers treat owner-builder projects differently.

Be cautious if a contractor asks you to pull the permit as an owner-builder. It can be a sign the contractor isn't licensed for the work, and it leaves you holding the risk.

Historic districts, HOAs, wildfire and flood zones

Some homes get extra review on top of the building permit.

Historic districts. Many cities have historic districts or landmark rules where exterior changes, especially those visible from the street, need design review before a permit is issued. Interior remodels are usually less affected.

HOAs. If you live in a community with a homeowners association, its rules and architectural review are separate from the building permit. A permit doesn't override HOA rules, and HOA approval doesn't replace a permit. Check both before you order materials.

Wildfire areas. Where homes meet wildland, some states and cities require wildfire-resistant materials for roofs, vents, windows and siding on new construction and many additions. California's fire hazard severity zones are one example. Some projects also need fire department review.

Flood zones. Homes in mapped flood zones can face extra rules on elevation and materials. Communities in the National Flood Insurance Program generally require a building to meet current flood rules when work counts as a substantial improvement, commonly defined as work costing half or more of the building's market value. Your local floodplain manager can tell you how that applies.

Hillsides and coasts. Steep lots can need grading permits, soils reports and engineered retaining walls. Coastal areas may have their own state or local review for some kinds of work.

Who pulls the permit

On most remodels, the contractor pulls the permit. Their license, where your state or city requires one, appears on the permit, they schedule the inspections, and they're responsible for getting the work signed off. That's the arrangement we recommend. When a project needs drawings, the designer or architect usually prepares the plan review package and answers corrections, often with the contractor's help.

Put the permit in your contract. It should say who applies for it, who pays the fees, and that the final payment is due after the final inspection passes. See how to hire a contractor for more on contracts.

Common questions

How long does it take to get a permit?

It varies. Over-the-counter permits for simple work can be issued the same day. Small projects with drawings can sometimes be approved quickly. Additions and ADUs going through full plan review commonly take several weeks to a few months, depending on the department and how many rounds of corrections are needed.

Do I need a permit to replace my kitchen cabinets?

Usually not, if the new cabinets go in the same place and no plumbing, electrical or walls change. Once you move the sink, add circuits or take out a wall, you need one.

Can I get a permit for work that's already done?

Often, yes. Many departments have a process to permit existing work, but expect the inspector to ask for walls or ceilings to be opened so the work can be seen, and for anything not up to code to be fixed.

Do I have to be home for inspections?

Usually not, as long as the inspector can get in and your contractor is there. Ask your contractor how they handle access.

Where do I find the rules for my town?

Start with your city or county building department's website. Many post which codes they've adopted, what work needs a permit, and how to apply. If you're in a rural area with no local department, your state's building codes office can tell you who handles permits.
